ABOUT

Research profile

Dr. Daniela Granato is a Brazilian scientist whose research focuses on dendrochronology, dendroclimatology, tree-ring analysis and climate reconstruction in tropical forests. She is currently Assistant Professor of Geography at the University of Arkansas, working at the interface of geosciences, forest ecology, climatology and environmental change.

Her scientific trajectory integrates biology, conservation, wood anatomy, forest ecology and tree-ring science. She has developed research using growth rings from native tropical species to reconstruct climate variability, investigate tree growth responses and improve understanding of long-term hydroclimatic dynamics in Amazonian and other tropical ecosystems.

Within GigaFlor and PELD-MIAG, Dr. Granato contributes as a scientific collaborator in dendrochronology and climate reconstruction, supporting the analysis of growth rings from giant Amazonian trees and associated species, including Cedrela odorata. Her expertise is essential for connecting tree age, growth history, climate sensitivity and long-term forest dynamics.

SCIENTIFIC APPROACH

Tree rings, climate memory and Amazon forest history

Dr. Granato's work uses tree rings as natural archives of environmental variability. By combining dendrochronology, wood anatomy, radiocarbon dating, climate data and ecological interpretation, her research helps reconstruct past rainfall variability, drought and flood extremes, and the climatic drivers of tree growth in tropical forests.

Her doctoral research focused on climate signals and rainfall reconstruction using Cedrela tree-ring chronologies from the eastern Amazon Basin. This work contributed to strengthening tropical dendroclimatology as a powerful approach for understanding climate variability in regions where long instrumental records are limited.

Her research has also contributed to broader scientific advances on Amazon precipitation, tree growth extremes, radiocarbon validation of annual rings in tropical trees and the role of dry-season climate variability in tropical tree growth.

GIGAFLOR CONTRIBUTION

Dendrochronology of giant Amazonian trees

In the GigaFlor network, Dr. Granato contributes to one of the most challenging and scientifically important questions in giant-tree research: how old are the largest trees of the Amazon, and how have they grown through past climate variability?

Her work supports the development of protocols for sampling and interpreting growth rings in giant trees, with special attention to Cedrela odorata and other species with dendrochronological potential. These analyses can help estimate tree age, reconstruct growth trajectories and evaluate how climatic variability has influenced the development of exceptional trees over decades or centuries.

By linking dendrochronology with forest ecology, climate reconstruction and long-term monitoring, her contribution strengthens GigaFlor's capacity to understand not only where giant trees occur, but also how they grew, how they responded to past droughts and floods, and how vulnerable they may be under future climate change.

FIELDWORK AND METHODS

Sampling the past to understand future forest resilience

Dendrochronology provides a unique window into the past functioning of tropical forests. Through careful field sampling, laboratory preparation, ring-width measurements and crossdating, growth rings can reveal how trees responded to seasonal climate, extreme droughts, floods and long-term environmental variability.

This perspective is highly relevant to Amazon giant forests because exceptional trees may store centuries of ecological information in their wood. In GigaFlor, dendrochronological analyses will help connect individual tree histories with broader questions of carbon storage, forest resilience, climate sensitivity and conservation of giant-tree ecosystems.
